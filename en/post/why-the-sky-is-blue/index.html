Why is the sky blue, and red at sunset?

Air molecules scatter short (blue) wavelengths of sunlight far more than long (red) ones. That single idea explains both the noon sky and the sunset.

Published Nov 22, 2026, 07:00 UTC · 3 min · Concept explainer

In a blink

  • Sunlight contains many colours; air scatters blue light much more strongly than red.
  • Scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction, so the whole sky looks blue.
  • At sunset light travels through more air, most blue is scattered away, and red and orange remain.

Why it matters

It is a clean example of how one physical rule (scattering depends on wavelength) explains very different everyday sights.

Sunlight meets air

Visible sunlight spans a range of wavelengths. Molecules in the atmosphere scatter shorter wavelengths more strongly than longer ones, a process called Rayleigh scattering.

Why not violet?

Violet scatters even more, but the Sun emits less of it, the upper atmosphere absorbs some, and our eyes are less sensitive to it. The mix we perceive looks blue.

At sunset

When the Sun is low, its direct light crosses a much longer path of air. More blue is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving proportionally more red and orange.

Move the Sun across the sky

Short path through air: blue is scattered everywhere, so the sky looks blue.

A longer path: the Sun looks a little yellower as more blue scatters away.

A very long path: most blue is gone from the direct beam, leaving red and orange.

Mind map

Noon

Short blue wavelengths are scattered in all directions.

  • Blue sky

Sunset

A long path through air removes more blue from the direct light.

  • Red and orange

Why not violet

Less violet from the Sun, some absorption, and weaker eye sensitivity.

  • Sky looks blue
